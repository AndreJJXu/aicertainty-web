What is said

Text or transcripts a person chooses to share, including explicit requests and their own descriptions of their experience.

Voice

How it sounds

A 2.4-second pause

The acoustic form of speech: pauses, pace, pitch, and the timing of conversational turns.

Example observations

  • Tempo and pause structure
  • Pitch and intensity patterns

↳ Interpretation boundary

A pause alone does not establish anxiety, dissatisfaction, or deception. Check the speaker’s explanation, context, and recording conditions.

Vision & Body

What is visible

Turning to a screen, then looking back

Visible facial movement, head direction, gestures, posture, and their changes within an interaction.

Example observations

  • Facial and bodily movement
  • Gaze direction and gesture timing

↳ Interpretation boundary

A smile does not necessarily mean happiness; looking away does not establish lost interest. Situation, culture, and individual differences matter.

Interaction

How interaction unfolds

Undo twice → “Give me an example”

Interaction records that a person allows, such as clicks, undo actions, steps, and explicit feedback.

Example observations

  • Turn-taking and response latency
  • Choices, corrections, and feedback

↳ Interpretation boundary

Repeated edits and waiting do not establish limited ability. Interpret an interaction with its task, situation, and the person’s feedback.

02 / THE INTERPRETATION LAYER

Meaning lives in context.

In this concept, context and time sit around the inputs. They help frame interpretation; they are not extra sensors.

◎

Context

The task, surroundings, relationship, culture, and context a person has chosen to share. A group pattern cannot replace their explanation.

Time

Sequence, duration, change, and an appropriate baseline. One instant is only one observation.

  1. 01

    Observe

    Describe the available signals.

  2. 02

    Contextualize

    Check context, missing information, and conflicts.

  3. 03

    Clarify

    Ask the person before acting.

03 / THE INFERENCE BOUNDARY

Signals are observations.
States are hypotheses.

Emotion, intent, and need belong to an interpretation layer. A responsible design would keep these inferences uncertain, explain their evidence, and allow the person to correct them.

LONG-TERM / DESIGN POSITION

More data does not automatically mean more certainty.

Our long-term direction is to connect human signals with evidence and uncertainty. This page proposes a vocabulary for that conversation, not a universal checklist: availability, purpose, consent, and evidence should determine which inputs belong in a particular system.
